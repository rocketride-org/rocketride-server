City libraries are changing what they lend. For most of the last century a library card meant books, newspapers and, later, records and films. Over the past ten years many branches have added things that have little to do with reading: sewing machines, power drills, telescopes, board games, cake tins and even fishing rods. Librarians describe these "libraries of things" as a natural extension of the same idea. People share an item that most of them need only now and then, and the community saves money and space.

The programs started small. A branch in one neighborhood might put a few tools on a shelf behind the desk and track them with the same system it used for books. When demand grew, some cities built separate tool libraries with their own staff and workshops. Others partnered with repair cafés, where volunteers teach visitors how to fix lamps, toasters and bicycles instead of throwing them away.

Costs are lower than many people expect. Most items are donated, and the most popular ones pay for themselves quickly because a single drill can be borrowed dozens of times a year. The biggest expenses are storage and the time staff spend checking returned items for damage. Several libraries now ask borrowers to watch a short safety video before taking home power tools.

Not everyone is convinced. Some local hardware stores worried that free lending would cut into their sales. In practice, several store owners say the opposite happened: borrowers who try a tool and like it often come back to buy their own, along with the supplies they need. Critics inside libraries raise a different concern. They fear that attention and budgets will drift away from books and literacy programs, which remain the core of the library's mission.

Usage numbers suggest the programs are here to stay. In cities that publish their data, items such as carpet cleaners, projectors and camping gear are often booked weeks in advance, and many branches report that tool borrowers include people who had not used the library in years. For librarians, that may be the most important result: a reason for new visitors to walk through the door, where they might also leave with a book.
